# NASA Space Apps submission, judging, and winner lessons

**Research date:** 2026-11-13
**Scope:** Official NASA Space Apps 2026 submission and judging requirements, official NASA risk context relevant to MuscleMap, and documented 2024–2025 Global Winner lessons. This report does not predict 2026 results and does not treat winner features as judging guarantees.

## 2026 requirements that can determine eligibility

The current **2026 Judging & Awards Guide** is the controlling source for the 2026 process. A project must respond to one official NASA Space Apps Challenge; all team members must be registered for the same Local Event or Universal Event and appear on the team Members tab; and no new members can be added after submissions close. Local Event teams must have at least one member, Universal Event teams at least two, and all teams may have no more than six members. The project page must contain all required fields. [1]

The 2026 submission must include a short demo in either a slide presentation with a seven-slide limit, including title and introduction slides, or a video presentation limited to 30 seconds. The project submission page and linked materials must use English. A video may use another spoken language only when it includes English subtitles. The team must submit through the Project tab before **Sunday, November 15, 2026 at 11:59 p.m. local time** for its Local Event; Universal Event teams use the Team Owner’s local time. The guide also requires the team to accept the submission/terms confirmations, including an originality and external-resource declaration. [1] The step-by-step submission page is the official **Project Submission Guide**. [2]

The guide states that Global-Judging eligibility also includes NASA’s review under its China funding-restriction policy. The relevant restriction is based on prohibited affiliation or bilateral activity with China or a Chinese-owned entity, including entities incorporated under the laws of China or its governmental subdivisions, including Hong Kong and Macau. NASA says eligibility determinations are made at its sole discretion based on the applicant’s information. [1] This is an eligibility gate, not a scored judging criterion.

## 2026 scoring and award path

At every judging level, projects receive a 1-to-5 score in five criteria: **Impact, Creativity, Validity, Relevance, and Presentation**. [1]

- **Impact:** the significance, reach, and effectiveness of the problem addressed and proposed solution.
- **Creativity:** originality, innovation, meaningful improvement, and inventive execution.
- **Validity:** scientific grounding, technical/functional/logical feasibility, completeness, usability, and real-world practicality.
- **Relevance:** direct response to the selected challenge and meaningful integration of NASA data as a core part of the solution rather than a superficial addition.
- **Presentation:** clarity, organization, delivery, storytelling, and the project’s ability to communicate the challenge, solution, and significance. Judges use both the demo and the full project.

For a top score, the guide’s descriptions are useful as a practical test. Impact should address a significant problem with broad or strongly evidenced benefit; Creativity should be notably original; Validity should be scientifically sound, feasible, usable, and ready or close to ready for real-world use; Relevance should make NASA data integral to the solution; and Presentation should be polished, compelling, and easy to understand. [1]

The process is staged. In **Phase 1**, Local Event and Universal Event judges select Global Nominees. Local judges are recruited and organized by the Local Lead; Universal Event projects are reviewed by subject-matter experts. Local awards are separate from Global Judging, and only officially submitted Global Nominees advance. The 2026 guide schedules Global Nominee announcements for **December 3, 2026**. [1]

In **Phase 2**, expert Global Judges review all Global Nominees using the same criteria and select Global Finalists. Honorable Mentions may be recognized for high-ranked ingenuity and creativity, but they do not advance to executive judging and are not eligible for the Global Awards. The guide schedules Global Finalists and Honorable Mentions for **December 17, 2026**. [1]

In **Phase 3**, an executive committee from NASA and the 2026 Space Agency Partners reviews the Global Finalists and selects ten award winners: Best Use of Science, Best Use of Data, Best Use of Technology, Galactic Impact, Best Mission Concept, Most Inspirational, Best Storytelling, Global Connection, Art & Technology, and Local Impact. The guide schedules the 2026 Global Winners announcement for **January 26, 2027**. [1] No 2026 winner should be claimed before that official announcement.

## 2026 originality, licensing, safety, and risk boundaries

The 2026 Terms and Conditions state that posted Solutions will be available for others to download or use. Original content created for Space Apps must be freely available without restriction or released under an OSI-compliant open-source license; if no specific OSI-compliant license is named, NASA says it will distribute the original content under Apache 2.0. [3]

Every external resource must be cited, including open-source or freely available code, text, images, and datasets. For non-NASA materials, the team is responsible for checking public availability or an OSI-compliant license, identifying applicable licenses in the submitted documentation, and ensuring that no export-controlled information is included. NASA says it may reject or remove materials without appropriate licensing documentation and may remove nominations, awards, or winner status when resources are not cited. [3] The submission should therefore have a complete References field and a clear separation between NASA material, permitted third-party material, and local demo fixtures.

The 2026 terms also exclude threatening, slanderous, obscene, discriminatory, sexually explicit, unlawful, commercial-promotional, proprietary/business-sensitive, unowned copyrighted, or personally identifying material. Recognizable names, voices, and likenesses of people under 18 must not appear in submissions; adults shown in submitted media require the stated permission. Video must be original or properly authorized, and NASA receives the specified broad, royalty-free government-use license for submitted video. [3]

The terms say participation and in-person attendance carry participant-assumed risk, and participants release NASA and the U.S. Government from claims associated with participation or submission. This is an event-liability provision, not permission to make a medical or operational safety claim. [3]

For MuscleMap specifically, NASA’s Human Research Program risk pages support a careful problem statement: NASA identifies reduced cardiorespiratory and musculoskeletal fitness as a human-spaceflight risk; microgravity can weaken bones, muscles, and cardiopulmonary systems, affecting critical tasks and injury risk. NASA also says ISS astronauts exercise and undergo periodic fitness self-evaluations, while individual effects can vary substantially. [4] [5] These sources support a mission-relevant monitoring and evidence-organization rationale. They do **not** establish a universal clinical threshold, individual diagnosis, treatment plan, causal exercise effect, or approved flight procedure. The demo should label measurements as observations or reference fixtures, preserve modality/phase/unit/source/missingness, and visibly distinguish evidence from interpretation.

## Lessons from officially documented earlier winners

The 2025 winners are the latest completed cycle documented on the official site, but they are **earlier-year evidence, not 2026 rules**. NASA’s 2025 announcement says ten winners were selected from more than 11,500 project submissions and judged by NASA and agency-partner subject-matter experts. It describes winners that turned open data into actionable tools. [6] The official 2025 winner-profile page separately says the winning teams were selected from 18,860 teams. These are different denominators reported by official pages; they should not be merged or presented as one submission count. [7]

The recurring lesson is not that a particular technology wins. It is that the project makes the NASA-data-to-user-value chain concrete and visible:

- **SpaceGenes+ (Best Use of Science, 2025)** presented an interactive dashboard for how radiation and microgravity together affect astronaut health at the molecular level. NASA’s profile emphasizes synergistic effects and accessible visualization. For MuscleMap, this supports showing why each field, phase, modality, or comparison exists instead of displaying an opaque health score. [6] [7]
- **Resonant Exoplanets (Best Use of Data, 2025)** built an AI system that ingests telescope and satellite data, including James Webb Space Telescope spectra, to identify exoplanets and possible biosignatures. NASA describes the goal as making signals more transparent and trustworthy. The transferable lesson is a traceable data pipeline and interpretable output, not simply adding AI. [6] [7]
- **Twisters / SkySense (Best Use of Technology, 2025)** combined NASA Earth-observation and weather datasets with a data pipeline, cleaning, probability metrics, and personalized risk windows. NASA’s description connects the technical pipeline to a clear everyday decision. For MuscleMap, show the transformation from source record to displayed observation and make uncertainty and provenance visible. [6] [7]
- **Astro Sweepers (Galactic Impact, 2025)** used public orbital data to produce debris-compliance reports and a risk index, while addressing operational, regulatory, and environmental concerns. The lesson is to connect a prototype to real users and decision contexts without hiding its assumptions. [6] [7]
- **PureFlow (Best Mission Concept, 2025)** let users design, model, and validate habitats against solar threats while considering power, waste, life support, and communications. The relevant lesson is systems thinking: demonstrate constraints and validation, not only a visually appealing interface. [6] [7]
- **HerCode Space (Best Storytelling, 2025)** combined NASA data and heliophysics concepts with illustrations and a classroom/outreach use case. MuscleMap’s short demo should similarly explain the problem, the evidence, the user action, and the boundary of what the product does in a single coherent story. [6] [7]
- **QUEÑARIS (Local Impact, 2025)** combined NASA satellite data with drones, AI, and local ecological knowledge to address water scarcity and forest restoration in Arequipa. This supports making MuscleMap’s intended user and operational setting specific rather than claiming generic global impact. [6] [7]

The 2024 winners reinforce the same pattern. NASA says the 2024 winners were selected from 9,996 project submissions by NASA and space-agency-partner subject-matter experts. [8] Examples include SkyShield’s use of real-time Near-Earth Object and meteor-stream data to identify satellite risk zones; Waterwise’s use of Earth-observation data for drought, pest, and disease-related farm water management; 2plant’s use of soil-moisture and evapotranspiration data to inform planting risk; Landsat Connect’s simple interface and pass-over notifications; and Connected Earth Museum’s guided, interactive 2D/3D environmental visualizations. [8] Taken together, the official descriptions point to five defensible practices: direct challenge alignment, NASA data used in the core workflow, a specific audience or decision, a working or testable interaction, and communication that makes complex evidence understandable.

## MuscleMap submission implications

For the 2026 submission, the strongest evidence package is a compact story that answers five questions in order: **What specific NASA challenge does MuscleMap answer? What human-spaceflight or research problem does it address? Which NASA evidence or data is actually used? What can a user do in the working prototype? What remains unavailable, unvalidated, or outside the product’s authority?** This ordering serves Relevance, Validity, Impact, and Presentation without overstating the evidence.

The demo should make NASA provenance visible at the point of use. A viewer should be able to distinguish an authorized or public source from a local reference fixture, see the modality and phase, inspect units and missingness, and understand whether a value is an acquired measurement, a reviewed derived observation, or a placeholder. Do not imply access to astronaut medical records, OnePortal records, raw ISS ultrasound files, or a NASA-approved medical workflow unless that access and authorization actually exist.

The safety wording should remain conservative. Public NASA risk pages can support the motivation and domain context. They should not be copied into a diagnostic, treatment, triage, or emergency-procedure feature. Avoid claims such as “prevents injury,” “clears a crew member,” “proves exercise preserved muscle,” or “NASA-approved.” Use language such as “supports longitudinal evidence review,” “organizes modality-specific observations,” and “prototype for authorized research or operations.”

Before submission, verify the seven-slide or 30-second limit, English/subtitle rule, complete fields, same-event team membership, and local-time deadline. Attach source and license information for every external resource. These are eligibility and compliance checks, not optional polish. Presentation then becomes the final scored criterion: the story should be brief, legible, honest about uncertainty, and demonstrably tied to the selected challenge.

## Limitations and distinctions

The 2026 guide is the applicable current rule set as of the research date. The 2024 and 2025 winner pages are retrospective examples and cannot establish 2026 eligibility, scoring weights beyond the current guide, or a guarantee that similar projects will win. [1] [6] [8]

Official NASA pages report different 2025 participation denominators: the NASA news release says more than 11,500 project submissions, while the official winner-profile page says 18,860 teams participated. The report preserves both claims and does not infer a conversion between them. [6] [7]

NASA’s risk pages describe population-level or program-level human-spaceflight risks and countermeasure research. They do not disclose a public participant-level MuscleMap dataset, authorize a medical decision-support product, or validate any project-specific algorithm. [4] [5]
