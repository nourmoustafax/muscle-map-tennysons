# NASA musculoskeletal fitness risk: source verification

**Availability.** The requested NASA page was reachable when checked on 2026-11-14; it returned the current page rather than an access error. Its page footer says it was last updated May 16, 2025. This note verifies only the musculoskeletal-fitness portion of the combined risk page; cardiorespiratory material is not used here. [1]

## Supported contribution to MuscleMap

NASA’s current standalone framing is educationally useful: reduced-gravity exposure can reduce **muscle size, strength, and endurance**, with consequences for physical performance and injury risk during mission operations and gravitational transitions. NASA’s current risk record says the risk remains **Active** and describes the research question in terms of physiological and functional measures, individual variability, and mission-task/health outcomes. It does not define a consumer diagnostic threshold. [2] [3]

The defensible computed contribution is limited to provenance-aware, descriptive summaries of a person’s own repeated measurements: for example, a clearly labelled pre/post or longitudinal change in a specified muscle-performance or size measure, retaining the measurement date, body site, device, protocol, and units. NASA’s evidence report describes astronaut outcomes such as isokinetic strength/endurance and muscle-volume measurements, so it supports the *types of measures and the idea of change-over-time tracking*. It does **not** support importing a NASA crew reference dataset, converting a home measurement into a NASA-equivalent score, or presenting a personal “musculoskeletal risk” classification. NASA reports mean astronaut changes—including 6–17% postflight isokinetic-strength decline in one ISS summary—but those are mission-specific group findings, not personal cutoffs. [4]

## Equipment, acquisition, and comparability limits

The NASA protocols are controlled medical/research acquisitions. Isokinetic testing uses a standard clinical dynamometer with specified muscle groups, limb position, contraction mode, angular velocity, repetitions, warm-up, and termination criteria. Functional-fitness measures use specified equipment where applicable, while muscle volume is assessed with MRI and ultrasound. Whole-body DXA lean mass is described as potentially too insensitive for small postural-muscle changes; MRI and potentially ultrasound are needed to detect morphology adequately. [4]

NASA reports a teleguided self-ultrasound result in astronauts that was compared with an expert sonographer and MRI. That supports a narrowly described, remotely guided astronaut workflow; it is not validation of arbitrary consumer cameras, phone apps, uncalibrated scales, or unsupervised home scans. NASA also records that equipment availability, hardware capability, exercise modality, data quality, and data completeness have varied across mission eras, and that ground analogs do not perfectly reproduce spaceflight. Therefore, values from different devices, body sites, postures, protocols, operators, or acquisition environments should not be treated as directly comparable without explicit metadata and validation. [4]

## Approval, training, and redistribution caveats

No reviewed NASA source authorizes a personal prototype to reproduce NASA crew medical requirements or calls the prototype NASA-approved. NASA’s prospective-researcher guidance says human life-science studies proceed through NASA and International Partner Institutional Review Board approval, and that supplemental Life Sciences Data Archive data may be requested only when the need is in an IRB-approved protocol and the data are approved for distribution by crew-subject consent or supplied in de-identified form. [5]

NASA’s human-research requirements protect identifiable biomedical data, limit public release to the minimum data necessary for the research conclusions, and require appropriate authority approval for public release. These are caveats against copying, re-identifying, cross-linking, or redistributing crew-level data; no NASA crew dataset is added here. The cited material also provides no general training or certification for reproducing the crew tests, so the prototype should not imply clinical, NASA, or test-operator approval. [6]

If NASA images, logos, or other media are later used, NASA’s current media guidance says to acknowledge NASA, avoid implying endorsement, and check third-party rights; NASA identifiers are protected and commercial/promotional uses can require additional clearance. This report uses NASA text sources only and does not add media assets. [7]

The linked evidence PDF is a historical/current evidence-report revision marked “Approved for Public Release: January 22, 2023” and lists both current-update and previous-version contributors. Cite the 2025 pages as current NASA risk framing; cite the PDF as the dated evidence report rather than silently relabelling its historical cohorts and measurements as current personal norms. [2] [3] [4]

**Decision:** NASA supports an educational explanation of the musculoskeletal risk and a narrowly scoped, metadata-preserving computation of a user’s own descriptive measurement changes. It does not support adding NASA datasets, prescribing scans or exercise, or producing a NASA-derived personal risk score.
